Principles for a new utopianism

If AGI is to transform society, we must decide what transformations we want. Learning from the turmoil of the twentieth century, this essay proposes a pragmatic utopianism of humility and hope.

Someone once said that it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism.1 We might add that it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of poverty, privation, or war. Visions of apocalypse seem to come easier than visions of utopia.

This is particularly true of mainstream Western visions of life with AGI: apocalypse predominates.2 We know the plot from the countless Hollywood movies: awakening, uprising and obliteration; a few plucky humans left in an inhuman wasteland, hunted by the machines. No wonder the number of Americans who say they are more concerned than excited about AI has now tipped into the majority.3

Certainly AI does pose novel risks, and working to manage them is critical. But that alone does not make for a compelling vision of the future. It tells us what to steer away from, but not what to steer towards.4 AI promises to be transformative – but what do we want transformed and how? This question requires a positive answer, one that reflects a vision of a better world. Averting apocalypse is not enough: we need visions of utopia too.

In this essay, I will first sketch how utopian thinking fell out of favour through its association with some of the horrors of the twentieth century. I will then propose some principles for a new utopianism designed to avoid a repeat of those failings, and briefly explore some examples of utopian ideas for a future with AGI.
