Generative models trained on the output of earlier models lose knowledge across generations, rare knowledge first. The remedies that work all bring in information from outside the loop, and the theory of model collapse treats that supply as given: a fixed archive, drawn from a fixed distribution. We argue that the supply is the problem, and that cultural evolution has the theory of it. We read model collapse as Rogers' paradox: a population that comes to rely on copying ends up no better off than one that never copied, because copies go stale unless someone keeps learning for themselves. We test the identification in three steps. First, Rogers' model, fitted only to fully synthetic training, predicts to within a few percentage points how much rare knowledge language models, from word trigrams to a pretrained transformer, retain under other mixtures of fresh data.
