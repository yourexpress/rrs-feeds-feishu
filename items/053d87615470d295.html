A language model can write the weights of a small MLP, CNN, RNN or GNN from a plain-language specification, with no data and no gradient step. We ask when such a network can be proved exact in the float32 arithmetic it actually runs in. A float-safety lemma makes exhaustive float32 enumeration a proof for any summation order, and a layered error bound covers networks whose weights are not short binary fractions. With dynamic programming, template checks, an SMT proof, breadth-first search and induction for weight-tied recurrences, this certifies 12 of 15 written networks (one after a single constant fix) on every input of a stated domain (up to $10^{16}$ inputs, or every graph or grid up to a size bound), in a minute of CPU or less each. The certificates also say where a network stops being exact: the shortest-path network is exact iff the distance is at most 4,096 and fails at the predicted hops. Thirty new specifications were each written by a fresh session of one model family (Claude Opus 5.5).
