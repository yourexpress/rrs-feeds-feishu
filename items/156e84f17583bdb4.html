Using an energy pixels approach to estimate the size a 2nd Generation DEL must be to train a high-resolution binding model

arXiv cs.LG · 2026-11-12 · 论文PDF

DNA-encoded libraries (DELs) are commonly used to find small-molecule binders to a target protein. But the hard part is no longer finding a hit. It is building a machine-learning model good enough to guide the medicinal chemistry that follows, one that can resolve the shift in affinity when a chemist changes a single atom. Such a model needs two things from its training data: dense sampling of the small patch of chemical space around the hit, and an adequate number of genuinely tight binders within it. A diverse naive DEL gives neither. It samples an enormous space, so any one neighborhood is covered far too thinly, and the tightest binders are rare. A model trained on that naive DEL data must extrapolate to binders tighter than any it has seen, and that is where models can fail. One way to get training data with both properties is a second-generation DEL built around the hit. We ask how complex that library must be. We treat binding as a sum of small, independent interactions, called pixels on the target and antipixels on the ligand.
