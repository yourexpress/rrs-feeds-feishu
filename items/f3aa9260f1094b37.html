Searching for distant planets with Astra

Two amateur astronomers used Codex and Astra to look for signs of planets in public telescope data. Now they’ve given professional astronomers a few places to look more closely.

Astronomers have confirmed more than 6,400 planets beyond our solar system(opens in a new window) since the first ones were announced in 1992(opens in a new window). A worldwide community of amateur astronomers has long been part of that search. Some comb through public telescope data for small, repeated dips in a star’s brightness that could signal an orbiting planet. Others use their own telescopes to watch for the next dip and share what they see with researchers.

Astronomers have used AI in this search for years. In 2017, researchers trained a neural network on 15,000 signals from NASA’s Kepler Space Telescope(opens in a new window) that astronomers had already examined. From those examples, the model learned to recognize which patterns in starlight were most likely to come from a planet. When the researchers used it to examine weaker signals, one led them to an eighth planet orbiting a star called Kepler-90(opens in a new window).

Now, tools like Astra are helping amateur astronomers design and run searches of their own, often on data that has already yielded the more obvious exoplanet signals. Kevin Bond, an engineer at OpenAI, came to astronomy through his love of photographing the night sky. He used Codex to search observations of about 100,000 stars from the Kepler telescope, a planet-hunting mission that ran from 2009 to 2018. Ethan Byrd, founder of the email infrastructure start-up Primitive, has always loved astronomy. Working independently of Bond, he tried several AI models before choosing Astra to help him analyze telescope data from about 19 million stars.
