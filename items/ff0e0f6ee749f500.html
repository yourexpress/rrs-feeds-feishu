Nano Banana 2.1

Google 经开发者 changelog 发布图像模型 Nano Banana 2.1，图像生成价格减半并在 API 中弃用旧版；官方 changelog 页本轮不可达，链接为可溯源的第三方报道

Google DeepMind’s launch artwork for Nano Banana 2.1. Image: Google DeepMind

Google has released Nano Banana 2.1, a new version of its everyday image generation and editing model, and for developers the headline is the price: a standard 1K image in the Gemini API now costs about $0.034, half what Nano Banana 2 charged. The model went on general availability on October 6 and is built on Gemini 3.6 Flash, according to its model card.

It replaces Nano Banana 2 (Gemini 3.1 Flash Image), which Google has now marked as deprecated in the API. Google did not publish a launch blog post; the details come from its developer changelog, pricing page, model card and the Nano Banana page on Google DeepMind's site.

What Google says is better

Google says Nano Banana 2.1 outperforms earlier Nano Banana models, with better "visual design, mask-based editing, subject consistency, and more natural-looking images". The API changelog adds improved prompt adherence, text rendering and character consistency across a multi-turn conversation, plus new wide and tall aspect ratios (1:4, 4:1, 1:8 and 8:1) at 1K, 2K and 4K.

The model card backs this up with Google's own human preference tests, scored on an Elo scale. With its thinking mode on, Nano Banana 2.1 scored 1050 overall against 990 for Nano Banana 2 and 935 for the more expensive Nano Banana Pro. The biggest gap is in infographics: on Google's automated factuality score it reached 0.521, against 0.179 for Nano Banana 2. These are Google's figures and have not been independently checked.

The card is also frank about what still goes wrong: small text that is "often blurry" at 1K, trouble with long paragraphs, imperfect character consistency between input and output images, and "occasional confusion" over left and right. Google's developer docs say Nano Banana models can mix up to 14 reference images into one picture, and that every generated image carries a SynthID watermark, which anyone can now check with Google's public SynthID Detector.
