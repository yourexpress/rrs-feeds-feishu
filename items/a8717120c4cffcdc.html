The hidden layers of Large Language Models (LLMs) are believed to encode representations of the data whose abstraction increases with depth. However, in the absence of an operational notion of abstraction, this statement is hard to test quantitatively. Here, we propose an alternative view, building on the principle that the correlations of a sequence reflect the structure of the process that generated it. We define the autocorrelation function of a layer as the average dot product between its activations at two context positions separated by a lag. Latent variables encoded persistently along the context create slowly decaying tails in the autocorrelation, and we find these tails to grow across layers in both synthetic and natural data. In synthetic data with controlled hierarchical latent structure, this growth stems from the encoding of abstract latent variables, which govern long stretches of the input. Indeed, the persistence of latent codes predicts the qualitative structure of autocorrelations and accounts for most of the empirical measures.
