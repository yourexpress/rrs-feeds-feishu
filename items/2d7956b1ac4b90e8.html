The Scope Grammar of Natural-Language Rules: Counting and Enumerating Readings modulo Logical Equivalence

arXiv · 2026-11-06 · 论文PDF

A rule written for people fixes its atomic conditions and connectives but leaves open how the connectives are bracketed, and each bracketing is a different logical reading that an autoformalizer must resolve or expose. We study the scope grammar, a deliberately ambiguous context-free grammar whose derivation trees are the readings of a connective skeleton and whose quotient by logical equivalence is what matters downstream. A skeleton with $k$ binary connectives has $C_k$ derivation trees, the $k$-th Catalan number, but far fewer logically distinct readings. For the positive, repetition-free fragment we make the gap exact: through the uniqueness of the read-once normal form we characterize the realizable normal forms by lowest common ancestors and count them with a two-label recurrence over the operator word, verified against exhaustive enumeration up to eleven atoms. The count is invariant under reversal and $\wedge/\vee$ duality; a skeleton is ambiguous if and only if it uses both $\wedge$ and $\vee$, a one-pass test; and the number of classes is at least $2^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}$.
