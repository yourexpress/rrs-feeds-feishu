We analyze DéjàVu, a simple protocol for plurality consensus: in each synchronous round, each agent samples one uniformly random agent and adopts the first opinion it observes twice, resetting its memory after every adoption. The rule requires neither frequency counters nor knowledge of the population size or the number of opinions. It relies only on repetition detection, an ability documented in single neurons and reflected in the mere-exposure effect. For a population of $n$ agents with $k$ initial opinions, let $M_0$ be the initial count of the plurality opinion (so $n/M_0 \le k$) and $Δ_0$ its bias, the difference between $M_0$ and the count of the second most popular opinion. We prove that, whenever $Δ_0 = Ω(\sqrt{M_0 \log n})$, DéjàVu reaches consensus on the initial plurality opinion within $O(\sqrt{n/M_0} \log(e M_0 / Δ_0) + \log\log n)$ rounds with high probability. Moreover, we show that, for the same bias regime, DéjàVu is asymptotically never slower than the $h$-majority protocol, collecting its $h$ samples one per round, for every fixed choice of $h$, even one chosen optimally for the initial configuration.
